What is an AI interview copilot?

An AI interview copilot listens during a live interview, understands the question the moment it is asked, and suggests an answer — in real time, on a screen only you can see. Think of it as a second brain sitting next to you: it knows your resume, it has read the job description, and it turns both into a short, structured answer before you have to speak.

It is not a chatbot you talk to between rounds, and it is not a resume writer. The whole point is live: the help arrives while the conversation is still happening.

How a live copilot works, step by step

  1. It listens. The copilot captures your computer's audio — the interviewer's voice coming through Zoom, Teams, Google Meet, or any call app. No meeting bot joins the call; nothing appears in the participant list.
  2. It transcribes. Speech-to-text turns the audio into a live transcript, so the copilot always knows what was just asked — and what was said five minutes ago.
  3. It reads your context. Before the call, you give it your resume and the job description. Good copilots match your real experience to the role, so the suggested answer names things you actually did, not generic advice.
  4. It answers. A language model drafts a short answer — usually a structure first (the point, the evidence, the number), then the wording. The best ones answer in under a second, because a slow answer is useless mid-conversation.
  5. It stays invisible. The answer appears in an overlay that is hidden from screen shares and recordings. The interviewer sees nothing.
  6. It takes notes. When the call ends, you get a summary, the full transcript, and action items — the meeting-notes half of the job.

What a copilot is good for

  • Behavioral questions. "Tell me about a time you…" — the copilot picks the strongest story from your resume and reminds you of the numbers in it.
  • Technical interviews. System design and coding rounds, where a structured approach (clarify scope, name trade-offs, then code) matters more than the first line of code.
  • Sales and support calls. Objections, discovery questions, and follow-ups — with your product knowledge loaded as context.
  • Meetings. Live notes, decisions, and follow-ups, so nothing agreed in the call gets lost afterwards.
  • Practice. Mock interviews are the safest place to use one: you get instant feedback on your answers without any stakes.

How to use one well

  • Load real context. A copilot is only as good as what you feed it. Paste the actual job description and a real resume — vague inputs produce vague answers.
  • Use it as a prompt, not a script. Read the structure, then answer in your own words. Reading a suggested answer out loud, word for word, sounds exactly like what it is.
  • Keep answers short. A 40-second answer beats a two-minute one. If the suggestion is long, take the first point and the one number that proves it.
  • Rehearse with it first. Run a mock interview before the real one. You will learn where it helps you and where it slows you down — and you will stop staring at the overlay.
  • Have a fallback. Mic permissions, network drops, and key errors all happen. Know your three best stories cold, with or without the tool.

What it cannot do

  • It cannot invent experience you do not have — and if it tries, the interviewer will find out in the follow-up question.
  • It cannot read the room: tone, hesitation, and what the interviewer actually cares about are still yours to judge.
  • It cannot save a bad fit. If the role is wrong for you, a polished answer only delays the discovery.

The ethics note — read this first

Many employers treat undisclosed live help during an interview as cheating, and some run detection tools that flag it. A copilot can also be against a company's interview policy even when no one is watching. Check the employer's or interviewer's policy before using one in a live interview. The safest uses — practice, mock interviews, preparation, and meetings where you are the one being helped to take notes — carry none of that risk. If a role says no assistance, respect it: getting the job under false pretences is a bad start, and getting caught is worse.
